## Topic 1 — Service Brand Value and Business Impact

**Focus:** Intangibility · trust · risk reduction · differentiation · commercial value

### Key concepts

- **Promise:** A service brand sets an expectation about the outcome and treatment customers should receive.
- **Experience:** Customers interpret the brand through people, processes, physical and digital evidence.
- **Trust:** Consistent proof lowers perceived risk before an intangible service can be evaluated.
- **Value:** A strong service brand can support preference, loyalty, referrals, resilience and price confidence.

### A service brand is a promise made visible through experience.

> **Key point:** A service brand is a promise made visible through experience. Identity attracts attention; repeated delivery earns belief.

### Why services need a different branding lens

- **Intangible:** Customers cannot inspect the full service before buying; cues and proof reduce uncertainty.
- **Inseparable:** Employees and customers often co-produce the experience in real time.
- **Variable:** Performance can change across people, channels, locations and moments.
- **Perishable:** Unused capacity cannot be stored; demand and access shape the experience.

### Value is co-created, not merely exchanged

#### The product era · goods-dominant logic

- Goods-dominant logic: value is built into the offering and realised at the point of sale.
- The customer is a passive recipient of what was produced.
- Attention sits on value-in-exchange — the price paid.

#### The service era · service-dominant logic

- Service-dominant logic: value is intangible and created with the customer in real time.
- The customer is an active partner in producing the outcome.
- Attention sits on value-in-use — the benefit actually realised.

### Perceived risk rises as tangibility falls

A continuum running from *Easy to evaluate · lower risk* to *Hard to evaluate · higher risk*.

- **Search qualities:** Can be judged before purchase by direct inspection, such as a room, a menu or a device.
- **Experience qualities:** Can be judged only after use, such as a meal, a stay, a course or a consultation.
- **Credence qualities:** Hard to judge even after use because the customer lacks the expertise, such as legal, medical or advisory work.

> **Why it matters:** As an offer moves toward credence, customers lean on risk relievers instead of inspection: standards, guarantees, credentials and brand reputation. That is the work a service brand does.

### How service-brand value is created

1. Clear promise
2. Credible signals
3. Consistent delivery
4. Positive memory
5. Trust and advocacy

### Benefits across the business

- **Customer:** Faster recognition, lower perceived risk and clearer reasons to choose.
- **Employee:** Shared standards and meaning guide judgement at moments that matter.
- **Commercial:** Preference, retention, referrals and premium confidence can strengthen economics.
- **Strategic:** A coherent brand focuses investment and makes expansion easier to govern.

### Brand identity and brand meaning

#### Perspective A

- Identity is what the organisation intends: name, promise, voice, design and standards.
- Management can define and communicate these signals.
- They create expectations before service use.

#### Perspective B

- Meaning is what customers and employees conclude from lived experience.
- It is shaped by outcomes, emotions, stories and recovery.
- It cannot be declared into existence.

### Each service characteristic sets a branding task

- **Intangibility → evidence:** Cannot be touched or inspected, so supply physical and peripheral cues that stand in for inspection.
- **Inseparability → interaction:** Produced and consumed at the same moment, so manage the customer–employee encounter directly.
- **Perishability → capacity:** Cannot be stored, so match supply to demand through scheduling, access and pricing.
- **Variability → standards:** Quality depends on human actors, so standardise the core process and invest in frontline capability.

### Sell the capability, not the component

#### What the evidence shows

- The object is a distribution mechanism; the customer buys the outcome it enables.
- A streaming service sells discovery and access, not ownership of recordings.
- A sportswear brand sells athletic progress through apps, community and tracking, not only footwear.

#### What it means for your brand

- Ask what capability the customer gains, not what the organisation hands over.
- Define the promise around the realised benefit so it survives changes in the delivery mechanism.
- Value emerges across an ecosystem of customers, employees, partners and digital tools.

### Three forms of service-brand proof

- **Performance:** Reliable outcomes, accuracy, timeliness and problem resolution.
- **People:** Competence, empathy, judgement and confidence in the promise.
- **Evidence:** Environment, content, reviews, cases, process visibility and guarantees.

### The 7Ps as brand-delivery levers

- **Proposition:** Product/service, price and promotion frame the value and expectation.
- **Access:** Place and channel determine convenience, inclusion and reach.
- **Delivery:** People and process convert the promise into customer outcomes.
- **Proof:** Physical evidence and digital cues make quality easier to judge.

### Customer decision journey

1. Recognise need
2. Search
3. Compare
4. Commit
5. Experience
6. Remember and share

### Sources of perceived risk

- **Functional:** Will it solve the problem?
- **Financial:** Is the outcome worth the price?
- **Time:** Will access or delay create loss?
- **Social:** What will the choice signal to others?

### Competitive advantage versus empty claims

#### Perspective A

- Distinctive promise tied to a valued customer outcome.
- Capabilities and standards competitors cannot easily copy.
- Evidence from consistent delivery and recovery.

#### Perspective B

- Generic superlatives such as best, trusted or world-class.
- Visual refresh without operational change.
- A promise employees cannot explain or deliver.

### Brand value chain

- **Input:** Strategy, research, talent, systems and service design.
- **Delivery:** Touchpoint performance, employee behaviour and recovery.
- **Response:** Awareness, trust, satisfaction, preference and advocacy.
- **Outcome:** Retention, referral, revenue quality and strategic resilience.

### Differentiation must be experienced, not merely described.

> **Key point:** Differentiation must be experienced, not merely described. The test is whether customers can name the difference and employees can reproduce it.

## Topic 2 — From Brand Strategy to Service Experience

**Focus:** Purpose · audience · positioning · promise · touchpoints · tangible cues

### Key concepts

- **Purpose:** Clarifies the valued outcome and role the organisation chooses to play.
- **Positioning:** Defines the audience, frame of reference, relevant difference and proof.
- **Promise:** Turns positioning into an expectation that can be translated into service standards.
- **Touchpoints:** Connect strategy to the moments before, during and after service delivery.

### Service-brand strategy sequence

1. Research audience
2. Audit market
3. Choose position
4. Write promise
5. Design proof
6. Govern delivery

### Audience insight

- **Jobs:** What progress is the customer trying to make?
- **Pains:** What risk, effort or uncertainty blocks progress?
- **Gains:** What outcome and feeling define success?
- **Context:** Who influences choice, use and renewal?

### Positioning choices

#### Perspective A

- Choose a specific priority audience and decision context.
- Name the valued difference and trade-off.
- Identify capabilities that make the claim credible.

#### Perspective B

- Trying to appeal equally to everyone weakens relevance.
- Copying category language creates sameness.
- A promise without operational proof raises disappointment risk.

### A practical brand promise

- **Relevant:** Connects to a meaningful customer outcome.
- **Distinctive:** Creates a recognisable reason to choose.
- **Deliverable:** Can be translated into behaviours, process and evidence.

### Promise-to-proof architecture

1. Promise
2. Principles
3. Service standards
4. Touchpoint cues
5. Evidence
6. Review

### Tangibilise the intangible

- **Show process:** Explain stages, timelines, owners and what happens next.
- **Show expertise:** Use credentials, cases, demonstrations and useful content.
- **Show outcomes:** Use specific evidence and customer stories with context.
- **Show assurance:** Set expectations, safeguards, access and recovery commitments.

### The service expansion of the marketing mix

- **Base layer:** Product, price, place and promotion frame the offer but cannot by themselves carry an intangible promise.
- **People:** The human actors who deliver the promise in real time and shape how it is remembered.
- **Process:** Operational flow, waiting, sequencing and how much the customer is asked to do.
- **Physical evidence:** The tangible and peripheral cues that signal the quality of something invisible.

### Physical space works as a behavioural stimulus

1. Ambient conditions
2. Space and function
3. Signs and symbols
4. Internal response
5. Approach or avoid

### Designing the servicescape

#### Stimulus · the environment

- Stimulus: ambient conditions, layout and equipment, signage, décor and brand livery.
- These cues act on customers and employees at the same time.
- They are read before anyone speaks, so they set the expectation the service must then meet.

#### Response · the behaviour

- Response: approach behaviour means explore, stay longer, engage and return.
- Avoidance behaviour means disengage, leave early and discount the promise.
- Digital environments carry the same load — layout, speed, clarity and tone are servicescape too.

### Service delivery as a staged performance

#### Front stage · visible

- Front stage is everything the customer can see: the setting, the interaction and the visible sequence.
- Frontline employees perform the promise, often doing emotional labour to hold it steady.
- The line of visibility marks where customer experience ends and operational mechanics begin.

#### Back stage · invisible

- Back stage is the support work, systems, training and management that make the front stage possible.
- Customers judge the front stage but failures usually originate behind the line.
- Blueprint both sides to find friction and fail points before customers do.

### Map moments that matter

- **Before:** Discovery, search, enquiry, comparison and booking.
- **During:** Welcome, diagnosis, delivery, waiting, handoff and payment.
- **After:** Onboarding, follow-up, support, renewal and advocacy.
- **Failure:** Recognition, ownership, recovery and learning.

### Standardise and personalise

#### Perspective A

- Standardise non-negotiable outcomes, safety, accuracy and brand-critical cues.
- Make handoffs and exception ownership visible.
- Measure whether the minimum promise is delivered.

#### Perspective B

- Personalise language, recommendation, pace and support to customer context.
- Give employees bounded judgement, not unlimited discretion.
- Protect privacy and fairness when data drives personalisation.

### Service blueprint layers

1. Customer action
2. Frontstage interaction
3. Backstage work
4. Support process
5. Evidence and metric

### Consistency without sameness

- **Outcome:** The core benefit remains dependable.
- **Character:** The brand feels recognisable across channels.
- **Context:** Delivery adapts to channel and customer need.
- **Control:** Exceptions are owned, logged and learned from.

### Relationship-building mechanisms

- **Access:** Make expert help easy to reach.
- **Continuity:** Remember context and reduce repeated effort.
- **Reciprocity:** Create useful exchanges beyond the transaction.

### Strategy becomes real at the next customer touchpoint.

> **Key point:** Strategy becomes real at the next customer touchpoint. If the promise cannot guide a design or operating decision, it is still too vague.

## Topic 3 — Champion Adoption and Deliver the Promise

**Focus:** Internal branding · leadership · employee enablement · culture · recovery

### Key concepts

- **Internal brand:** Employees need shared meaning, capability and reinforcement—not slogans alone.
- **Leadership:** What leaders fund, model, recognise and correct signals the true brand.
- **Enablement:** Training, tools, decision rights and feedback turn intent into repeatable action.
- **Recovery:** A well-owned failure can restore confidence and reveal where the system must improve.

### Internal brand adoption

1. Understand
2. Believe
3. Know how
4. Have authority
5. Act
6. Learn and reinforce

### Employee brand alignment

- **Meaning:** Explain who the service is for and why the promise matters.
- **Role:** Show how each function affects the customer outcome.
- **Capability:** Practise the behaviours and judgement required.
- **Reinforcement:** Align measures, recognition, coaching and consequences.

### Internal communication and internal branding

#### Perspective A

- Communication distributes messages and information.
- Awareness is necessary but not sufficient.
- Campaigns fade if systems reward different behaviour.

#### Perspective B

- Internal branding aligns culture, capability and operations with the promise.
- Employees test the promise against daily reality.
- Leaders must resolve contradictions.

### In a co-created service, the employee is the brand.

> **Key point:** In a co-created service, the employee is the brand. The gap that damages brands is between the promise marketing makes and the delivery employees execute at the moment of truth.

### Five inputs that build brand commitment

- **Internal communication:** Top-down clarity about the promise combined with bottom-up feedback that is actually used.
- **Market information:** Exposing staff to real customer feedback and competitor behaviour, not filtered summaries.
- **Brand training:** Moving past generic service skills to teach the brand-specific behaviours this promise requires.
- **Brand leadership:** Managers acting as visible role models, especially when the promise is costly to keep.

### Knowledge alone does not produce performance

- **Brand knowledge** (Cognitive): I know what the brand stands for. Necessary, but on its own it changes nothing.
- **Brand commitment** (Psychological bond): I believe in the brand and want it to succeed — an emotional attachment, not just compliance.
- **Brand performance** (Behavioural output): I deliver the promise consistently at the moment of truth, including when it is inconvenient.

### Leader actions that build credibility

- **Model:** Use the language and standards in real decisions.
- **Enable:** Remove barriers and fund brand-critical capabilities.
- **Reinforce:** Recognise evidence and address promise-breaking behaviour.

### Brand champion network

- **Sponsor:** Owns strategic commitment and cross-functional barriers.
- **Champions:** Translate the promise into local context and collect feedback.
- **Managers:** Coach performance and manage exceptions.
- **Employees:** Deliver, observe, recover and improve the experience.

### Behaviour design

1. Critical moment
2. Desired customer feeling
3. Observable behaviour
4. Tool or standard
5. Evidence
6. Coaching

### Empowerment guardrails

- **Purpose:** The outcome employees should protect.
- **Limits:** Safety, privacy, legal and financial boundaries.
- **Authority:** Decisions and resources available without escalation.
- **Escalation:** Clear route when needs exceed authority.

### Storytelling with integrity

#### Perspective A

- Use specific customer and employee moments.
- Connect the action to the promise and outcome.
- Include tension, judgement and evidence.

#### Perspective B

- Avoid invented testimonials or unsupported hero stories.
- Do not turn systemic failures into individual blame.
- Protect confidentiality and consent.

### Service-recovery loop

1. Recognise
2. Own
3. Stabilise
4. Resolve
5. Restore confidence
6. Learn

### Recovery dimensions

- **Outcome fairness:** A reasonable fix, replacement or remedy.
- **Process fairness:** Low effort, timely handling and clear ownership.
- **Interaction fairness:** Respect, empathy and credible explanation.
- **Learning:** Cause, action owner and prevention evidence.

### Adoption evidence

- **Leading:** Training completion, confidence, tool use and coaching.
- **Behaviour:** Observed standards, handoff quality and recovery ownership.
- **Outcome:** Customer experience, rework, retention and referrals.

### Employees cannot deliver a promise the organisation does not enable.

> **Key point:** Employees cannot deliver a promise the organisation does not enable. Internal branding is the operating system behind the external claim.

## Topic 4 — Evaluate and Improve Service-Brand Impact

**Focus:** Measurement system · KPI logic · journey evidence · diagnosis · improvement

### Key concepts

- **System:** Use a balanced chain of measures rather than one headline score.
- **Causality:** Connect employee and operational drivers to customer and business outcomes.
- **Segmentation:** Averages hide differences by journey, channel, audience and recovery status.
- **Action:** Every measure needs a decision, owner, threshold and review cadence.

### Brand-impact measurement chain

1. Capability
2. Delivery behaviour
3. Customer response
4. Commercial outcome
5. Learning

### Four measurement lenses

- **Customer:** Awareness, consideration, trust, effort, satisfaction and advocacy.
- **Employee:** Understanding, belief, confidence, enablement and observed behaviour.
- **Operational:** Accuracy, wait, resolution, handoff, recovery and consistency.
- **Commercial:** Conversion, retention, referral, share of wallet and revenue quality.

### Leading and lagging indicators

#### Perspective A

- Leading: training, confidence, staffing, standard adherence and response time.
- They provide earlier signals and can be influenced directly.
- They do not prove customer or commercial impact alone.

#### Perspective B

- Lagging: trust, retention, referrals, complaints and revenue outcomes.
- They confirm accumulated results but arrive later.
- They may be affected by price, seasonality and market conditions.

### Common customer measures

- **NPS:** Relationship or journey advocacy; pair with reasons and behaviour.
- **CSAT:** Satisfaction with a defined interaction or outcome.
- **CES:** Effort required to complete a task or resolve an issue.

### Qualitative evidence

- **Interviews:** Explore meaning, trade-offs and unmet needs.
- **Observation:** See actual behaviour and friction in context.
- **Reviews:** Detect repeated language, moments and expectations.
- **Complaints:** Reveal failure patterns, ownership and recovery quality.

### Build a useful KPI

1. Decision
2. Metric definition
3. Data source
4. Baseline and target
5. Segment
6. Owner and cadence

### Measurement quality checks

- **Valid:** Represents the construct or outcome claimed.
- **Reliable:** Produces comparable results under defined conditions.
- **Timely:** Available before the decision window closes.
- **Actionable:** Has a clear owner and response threshold.

### Correlation and contribution

#### Perspective A

- Track whether brand-related changes move with experience and business results.
- Use journey and segment comparisons to narrow explanations.
- State uncertainty and alternative drivers.

#### Perspective B

- Avoid claiming one campaign caused all commercial change.
- Use experiments, phased rollout or matched comparison where feasible.
- Triangulate quantitative and qualitative evidence.

### Dashboard design

- **Promise:** The customer outcome the dashboard tests.
- **Signal:** A small set of driver and outcome measures.
- **Context:** Target, trend, segment and notable change.
- **Action:** Owner, decision, due date and expected effect.

### Insight-to-improvement cycle

1. Detect
2. Diagnose
3. Prioritise
4. Experiment
5. Verify
6. Standardise or stop

### Evaluation cadences

- **Operational:** Daily or weekly exceptions and recovery.
- **Journey:** Monthly performance and qualitative themes.
- **Strategic:** Quarterly position, portfolio and investment choices.

### The architecture of value actualisation

Three reinforcing nodes producing seamless service performance — The output

- **The philosophy — Service-dominant logic:** Shift focus from selling objects to co-creating value-in-use with the customer.
- **The engine — Internal branding:** Align, equip and empower the people who deliver the promise.
- **The stage — Servicescape and process:** Design the physical, digital and operational environment that guides behaviour.

### Four strategic imperatives for an intangible economy

- **Sell outcomes, not objects:** Define the proposition around the capability enabled, not the item distributed.
- **Blueprint the theatre:** Map front-stage interaction and back-stage process to remove friction and locate fail points.
- **Use the environment:** Treat physical and digital space as a signal of quality that shapes what customers do.
- **Build from the inside out:** Fund internal capability as seriously as external marketing; employees are the brand asset.

### A metric is useful only when it changes a decision.

> **Key point:** A metric is useful only when it changes a decision. Measure the promise, diagnose the experience and close the improvement loop.
